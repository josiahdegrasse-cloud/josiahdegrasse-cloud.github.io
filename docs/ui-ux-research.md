# Evidence and craft in interface design

A strong portfolio must create interest, explain its subject, and make evidence easy to inspect. Research supports treating those as related but distinct goals. Attractive interfaces can influence perceptions and sometimes performance; visual appeal alone cannot establish that a visitor understood a project or completed a task. The design recommendation is therefore to combine a recognizable navigation structure with distinctive typography and unusually clear presentation of the work.

The resulting direction for Josiah deGrasse’s portfolio is an editorial catalogue: a quiet background, expressive display typography, compact project identifiers, substantial product imagery, and short explanations of design decisions. This is an art-direction judgment informed by research, not a scientifically established optimal style. Its success should be assessed through both visual evaluation and realistic portfolio-reading tasks.

## Scope and evidence quality

The evidence below covers visual first impressions, aesthetics and performance, reading, information seeking, and human–AI interaction. It includes 13 scholarly publications, supplemented by the W3C accessibility standard, current performance guidance, and the relevant employer’s role description. Foundational studies remain useful for mechanisms, while newer work qualifies their application. None of the studies directly tests this portfolio or predicts an OpenAI hiring decision.

The collection is a focused evidence review rather than an exhaustive systematic review. Primary publications, author copies, university repositories, and publisher records form its basis. Full text was accessible for several central papers; some claims rely on publisher abstracts or indexed original-paper excerpts where full text was unavailable. Bibliographic dates refer to publication, not search-engine crawl dates. The June 2026 meta-analysis is treated as a recent synthesis with acknowledged limitations, not as independently replicated proof of a universal effect.

Three distinctions prevent overclaiming. A person’s rating of usability differs from observed task performance; a screenshot impression differs from interaction; and a group average differs from a guarantee for an individual. Aesthetic preference also differs from reading effectiveness. Recommendations below identify which claims come from research and which are proposed applications.

## Visual impressions and experienced usability

Tractinsky, Katz, and Ikar studied an ATM-like application and found a strong relationship between perceived aesthetics and perceived usability before and after use. Their manipulation of aesthetics influenced subsequent perceptions. This is evidence that presentation matters to judgment, but it does not establish that attractive interfaces always improve task completion or repair poor information architecture. The application and experimental setting also differ substantially from a modern responsive portfolio. [1](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095354380000031X)

Tuch and colleagues’ website experiments examined visual complexity and prototypicality using short screenshot exposures. Lower-complexity, more typical pages tended to receive higher aesthetic ratings, with effects appearing very early. Those exposure durations concern perceptual judgments, not the time needed to understand a case study. The practical inference is to make the page’s purpose and hierarchy recognizable immediately, then place originality in typography, composition, and evidence selection. It is not a reason to make every portfolio look identical. [2](https://research.google/pubs/the-role-of-visual-complexity-and-prototypicality-regarding-first-impression-of-websites-working-towards-understanding-aesthetic-judgments/)

A separate Tuch study manipulated aesthetics and usability in an online shop with 80 participants. It did not find that aesthetics improved perceived usability in that experiment; instead, poor usability reduced post-use aesthetic ratings. The contrast with the ATM study makes context and manipulation strength important. An elegant surface should therefore be evaluated again after someone tries to find a project’s role, decisions, and outcome. [3](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563212000908)

Thielsch, Engel, and Hirschfeld tested 57 participants and distinguished screenshot-based expectations from post-interaction ratings and objective measures. Expected usability did not significantly predict experienced usability or task performance in their experiment; it was closely related to aesthetic ratings. This provides a concrete reason to separate visual review from task-based usability checks. A favorable screenshot review is useful evidence of appearance, not sufficient evidence of usability. [4](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-19/)

Two meta-analyses put the disagreement in perspective. The 2019 synthesis covered 25 studies and 101 outcomes and estimated a small positive performance effect, with substantial heterogeneity. [5](https://www.uni-muenster.de/imperia/md/content/owms/2019_thielsch_et_al.pdf) A preregistered 2026 synthesis included 31 studies, 234 effect sizes, and 18,794 participants; it reported an average effect of g = 0.29 and high unexplained heterogeneity. Its eligibility criteria required successful aesthetic manipulations, and its abstract identifies device, typography, and possible prior-experience confounding as relevant considerations. The samples and methods differ, so the estimates should not be presented as a simple trend over time. Neither standardized effect is a percentage improvement for this portfolio. [6](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10447318.2026.2664081)

**Design implication.** Preserve obvious Work, About, Résumé, and contact routes. Use a confident typographic opening, but include real work near the top rather than a long spectacle before the content. Evaluate the first screen for orientation and the case studies for comprehension. The portfolio may look accomplished while its evidence remains incomplete; those are separate editorial problems.

## Fluency, novelty, and visual character

Reber, Schwarz, and Winkielman propose that processing fluency contributes to aesthetic pleasure, reviewing relationships involving contrast, repetition, symmetry, and prototypicality. Their account is a theoretical synthesis rather than a controlled test of a particular website layout. It supports examining whether a composition is easy to parse, but it does not prove that minimalism, symmetry, or familiarity is always preferable. Fluency is a useful lens alongside the audience’s expertise and the content’s purpose. [7](https://psy2.ucsd.edu/~pwinkiel/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)

Moshagen and Thielsch developed the Visual Aesthetics of Websites Inventory across seven studies. Their model distinguishes simplicity, diversity, color, and craftsmanship as related facets of perceived aesthetics. That is more useful than treating beauty as a single decorative attribute. A page can feel orderly yet monotonous, or original yet poorly integrated. The instrument measures perceived aesthetics; its dimensions are not fixed layout specifications or a conversion formula. [8](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581910000777)

**Design implication.** Establish a stable grid, repeat label and caption treatments, and use a restrained palette. Introduce variety through contrasts in scale, the character of the type, and the different material qualities of the projects. The NFI product screen, Red Hat concept comparison, and lacrosse photograph provide distinct visual material without requiring unrelated decoration. Keep display typography expressive and body typography quieter.

The palette of near-white, dark brown-black, and oxblood is an editorial choice. So are the oversized serif heading and the catalogue-like figure labels. Research does not establish these colors as more intelligent, prestigious, trustworthy, or suitable for OpenAI. Their justification is compositional: they create a consistent identity while leaving real project interfaces visually legible. The desired sophistication comes from deliberate selection and spacing rather than claims of exclusivity.

**Boundary.** Novelty should not change how a visitor must learn to operate the site. An unusual cursor, concealed navigation, mandatory introductory animation, or horizontal scrolling narrative would need a clear task benefit. No such benefit is established here. A familiar interaction vocabulary leaves more attention available for the work itself.

## Information architecture and evidence finding

Pirolli and Card’s information-foraging framework models how people seek useful information while managing the cost of finding and consuming it. Information scent describes cues that help people estimate whether a path is likely to lead to something valuable. The work is a theoretical and computational account of information behavior, not a prescribed portfolio template. Its practical relevance is that project labels and summaries should predict what a visitor will find after clicking. [9](https://citeseerx.ist.psu.edu/document?doi=59f1e446fab201399d84ae48156ea7bd05c4439b&repid=rep1&type=pdf)

**Design implication.** Each featured project should expose its name, domain, design question, and evidence basis before the visitor commits to a long case study. “Read the case study” sits beside that context. A compact project index offers direct access to either main project, and ordinary internal anchors allow visitors to jump to research, design decisions, or testing.

Inside the case studies, a short overview answers three questions: what changed, what Josiah contributed, and what state the work is in. The longer narrative follows with supporting detail. This is a proposed reading architecture, not a claim about a universal recruiter attention span. It accommodates both a quick assessment and a deeper review without inventing an arbitrary reading-time promise.

The images should function as evidence. A screen caption identifies what is being shown and whether the data is synthetic. A reconstructed concept remains labeled as such. Enlargement allows readers to inspect detail, with a native dialog and an explicit close control. The image, caption, and nearby design explanation should jointly answer why the artifact matters.

**Content constraint.** A statistic without its context should not become a large promotional badge. The confirmed NFI task-completion statement still lacks participant count and protocol detail. The Red Hat interview and opportunity counts are confirmed, while original research and Figma artifacts remain pending. The homepage can communicate the scope of the work without turning those gaps into stronger claims.

## Typography and reading

Dyson’s review examines line length, columns, window size, and interlinear spacing in screen reading. It identifies methodological differences between studies and emphasizes characters per line as an important variable, while calling for further work on interactions among layout, eye movements, scrolling, reading patterns, and familiarity. It does not support a universal optimum of exactly 66 characters per line. Its older display context also limits direct transfer to contemporary phones. [10](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01449290410001715714)

Wallace and colleagues studied reading with different fonts, normalizing perceived size and measuring speed and comprehension in remote studies. Their results indicate substantial differences between individuals; preference did not reliably identify the most effective font. The often-cited 35% comparison concerns participants’ fastest and slowest tested fonts, not a promised gain from adopting one typeface. The author preprint is dated 2021; the final TOCHI publication appeared in 2022. [11](https://shaunwallace.org/files/Readability__TOCHI.pdf)

**Design implication.** Use Instrument Serif for large, brief statements and project titles; use DM Sans for navigation, summaries, and long-form explanation. This pairing is a craft decision, not a claim that either family is universally more readable. Maintain comfortable body sizes, avoid excessively long text blocks, and preserve browser zoom and text reflow. Supporting labels may be smaller, but they must remain legible and sufficiently contrasted.

The implementation keeps prose to a moderate measure, uses a distinct caption style, and gives headings enough separation to clarify structure. Local font files remove an external stylesheet request and keep the chosen type available with the site’s own assets. Font-display behavior retains fallback text while fonts arrive. Rendering quality still needs verification at narrow widths and enlarged text settings.

## Human–AI interaction and portfolio credibility

Amershi and colleagues synthesized 18 human–AI interaction guidelines and evaluated them through multiple rounds, including 49 design practitioners assessing 20 AI-infused products. The guidelines address such concerns as communicating capability and limitations, supporting correction, and enabling user control. They are design guidance evaluated for relevance and applicability, not evidence that any particular portfolio project has met a safety or performance standard. The 2019 product context also predates current generative-AI interfaces. [12](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Guidelines-for-Human-AI-Interaction-camera-ready.pdf)

Buçinca, Malaya, and Gajos tested cognitive forcing interventions in an AI-assisted decision task with 199 participants. The interventions reduced overreliance relative to simple explanation approaches, but the most effective designs received less favorable subjective ratings. Benefits also differed with participants’ motivation for effortful thinking. The finding challenges the assumption that more explanation or less friction is always better. It does not justify adding interruptions to every workflow. [13](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca2021trust.shtml)

**Design implication.** The strongest common thread in the NFI and Red Hat studies is inspectable assistance: show supporting evidence, identify what the system proposes, and leave consequential judgment visible. The portfolio should make that design argument explicit and then point to the actual relevant interface. A short statement of practice links directly to NFI’s human-control section so a visitor can inspect the work behind the claim.

The research strengthens the interpretation of the work, but it must not be retroactively represented as research conducted during the original projects. Neither the 2026 review nor these cited experiments were user studies of Josiah’s interfaces. The existing case studies should continue to distinguish project research, present-day interpretation, and unavailable artifacts.

## Accessibility and implementation quality

WCAG 2.2 supplies testable accessibility criteria rather than aesthetic advice. Relevant requirements include keyboard operation, visible focus, meaningful structure, text alternatives, contrast, reflow, and focus that is not entirely obscured. Its AA target-size criterion uses 24 CSS pixels with specified exceptions; 44-pixel targets are a useful implementation choice here rather than a universal AA requirement. Meeting selected checks is not equivalent to a complete conformance audit. [14](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)

Current Core Web Vitals guidance distinguishes loading, interaction responsiveness, and visual stability through LCP, INP, and CLS. The good thresholds are LCP at or below 2.5 seconds, INP at or below 200 milliseconds, and CLS at or below 0.1, assessed at the 75th percentile of real visits. Local browser observations cannot establish those field outcomes, and a low-traffic private portfolio may not have a useful public field dataset. [15](https://web.dev/articles/vitals)

**Design implication.** Keep semantic links and headings, visible keyboard focus, a working skip link, predictable dialogs, and adequate touch areas. Do not depend on motion or hover to reveal essential information. Reserve image dimensions, keep photographic assets compressed, and avoid loading the optional 3D experiment on the editorial routes. Use server-produced HTML for the primary portfolio content and verify direct navigation to each route.

The mobile version should be composed for its width. It should not merely shrink the desktop spread. Project summaries and imagery stack, the large display heading scales down, and captions wrap normally. The desktop case-study navigation can remain available while reading; at small widths it should avoid occupying an excessive share of the screen.

## Application to an AI-engineering portfolio with strong interface craft

The OpenAI Web Designer role describes work on web experiences, with emphasis on information architecture, user flows, responsive design, visual craft, design systems, accessibility, performance, and collaboration. It also values validation and iteration. This makes the portfolio website itself relevant evidence, but it does not replace a case study demonstrating those activities. The employer’s description is a role requirement, not research on selection outcomes. [16](https://openai.com/careers/web-designer-new-york-city/)

An AI-engineering presentation should make implementation tangible: the working product, system boundaries, retrieval behavior, review controls, and specific contribution matter more than a title change. The design principles above still apply, while technical assertions require project documentation rather than aesthetic inference.

The NFI project provides real interface material and a coherent design problem. Red Hat contributes enterprise research and divergent concepts, but the missing original artifacts weaken its visual evidence. Supporting physical and music projects communicate range and personal character. The hierarchy should reflect the strength and relevance of the evidence rather than present every project as equally mature.

| Design decision | Evidence-informed purpose | Concrete application | Remaining uncertainty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expressive opening with plain role statement | Support orientation and visual character | Large serif headline plus a concise description of the work | Visual appeal with target reviewers |
| Direct index to two main studies | Reduce the cost of finding relevant work | Named NFI and Red Hat links near the opening | Which project reviewers seek first |
| Project description beside artifact | Make a link’s destination and value clearer | Design question, scope, and actual screen presented together | Whether readers understand the contribution |
| At-a-glance case summaries | Support selective reading | Shift/question, contribution/evidence, current state/outcome | Whether summaries omit anything decision-critical |
| Visible artifact provenance | Keep evidence interpretable | Demo-data and reconstruction captions | Missing original source artifacts |
| Conventional controls and reduced motion | Support reliable operation | Native links, menu, image dialog, focus states | Assistive-technology testing beyond automated inspection |
| Moderate text measure and local fonts | Support reading and robust delivery | Separate display and prose treatments | Individual reading preferences and field performance |

## Evaluation plan

Visual judgment and task success should be assessed separately. First ask prospective reviewers what the page appears to be about and what kind of designer it presents, without leading them toward the intended answer. Then let them interact with the site and answer concrete questions about one project. Record misunderstandings, navigation detours, and the specific evidence they use.

A practical formative round could include five to eight people with relevant design-review or hiring experience, plus participants who use different access methods where feasible. This is a proposed starting sample for identifying problems, not a statistically representative estimate or a guarantee that all problems will be found. Use both phone and desktop contexts. Keep the questions neutral and preserve observations that contradict the intended positioning.

| Task | Observable success | Diagnostic failure |
|---|---|---|
| Identify Josiah’s design focus | Describes Human Factors and AI/technical interface work in their own words | Assumes the site represents an agency or unrelated discipline |
| Find the strongest example of decision-interface design | Opens NFI and identifies a relevant screen | Chooses based only on an unexplained acronym or decorative image |
| Explain one design decision | Connects a problem, response, and supporting artifact | Repeats a slogan without understanding the change |
| Distinguish evidence from illustration | Recognizes demo data and reconstructed concepts | Treats a sample product score as a project outcome |
| Inspect a detailed image and return | Enlarges, reads, and closes using their preferred input | Loses position, focus, or the close control |
| Find résumé and contact | Reaches the intended destination without searching unrelated content | Misses navigation or misunderstands link behavior |

After interaction, collect a short aesthetic assessment using an established instrument such as VisAWI-S if its wording and scoring are followed correctly. Compare that with observed comprehension and navigation behavior rather than blending all outcomes into a single invented quality score. Low traffic makes casual A/B conversion claims especially unreliable; a documented formative study is a more appropriate first step. No participant results should be reported until the sessions occur.

## Outstanding priorities

The most consequential next improvement is original project evidence. Obtain Red Hat research synthesis, the two Figma concepts, and a record of the validation discussion. Complete the NFI participant count, study protocol, task list, and specific changes following testing. A verified before-and-after account will strengthen the application more than additional decorative treatment.

A separate web-focused case study would address the role more directly if it documents information architecture, responsive visual design, prototype decisions, accessibility, and evaluation. It should be clearly identified as an independent concept if it is not commissioned work. It should not imply employment, endorsement, access to confidential research, or shipped outcomes. The portfolio’s own redesign can also become evidence when supported by real review sessions and documented revisions.

The current visual direction should remain a testable proposition. Preserve what helps visitors recognize the designer, understand the work, and inspect its basis. Revise parts that slow comprehension even when they look impressive in isolation. The distinction between evidence and taste is a source of design credibility, not a limitation to conceal.
